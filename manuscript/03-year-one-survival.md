# Year One Survival

## 0. Read This First: The Missing System

Welcome to CUHK Information Engineering.

This chapter does not replace official information. You still need to read study plans, course outlines, regulations, academic honesty rules, and department announcements.

But official information has a limit.

It tells you what the system says.  
It rarely tells you how the system feels when you are inside it.

Year One is where many students discover this gap.

The university gives you courses, lectures, tutorials, labs, assignments, exams, deadlines, and freedom. What it does not always give you is a complete system for surviving them.

It may teach you programming syntax, but not a practical programming workflow.  
It may give you lectures, but not all lectures are worth your time.  
It may give you freedom, but not the discipline required to survive that freedom.

That missing system is your responsibility.

The real purpose of Year One is not to become an expert immediately. It is to build the personal infrastructure that lets you survive and grow later.

> The university gives you content.  
> You need to build the workflow.



## 1. Programming Is Not Just Syntax

Year One programming courses may teach you variables, loops, arrays, functions, and basic problem solving. These are necessary. They are not enough.

Programming is often introduced as a course, not as a working environment. You may be taught how to use CLion, press the run button, and submit labs in ENGG1110. That is a start. It is not the whole world.

Real programming also includes:

- setting up a practical editor such as VS Code
- using the command line
- compiling and running programs manually
- using Git and GitHub
- debugging systematically
- reading compiler errors
- organising files properly
- using SSH and remote machines
- understanding basic project structure

These are not advanced bonus skills. They are survival skills.

CLion is useful. But if your entire workflow depends on one green run button, your foundation is incomplete.

Sooner or later, that button will disappear. You will meet a Linux machine, a remote server, an open-source project, a research codebase, or a broken environment where nobody cares that “it works on my IDE”.

If the course does not teach these things, learn them yourself.

By the end of Year One, you should be able to:

- write and run a small C or Python program outside an IDE
- use basic terminal commands
- use Git to track your work
- read simple compiler errors
- debug with some method, not only panic
- understand what SSH is used for
- organise a small project without losing files

This topic will be expanded in the next chapter, **Programming Foundation**.

> Programming is not just syntax.  
> It is environment, workflow, tools, and thinking.

Even if AI can write code, you still need the foundation to understand, test, debug, and maintain it.

## 2. Learning Requires Output

A dangerous illusion in university is thinking that input equals learning.

Input feels productive: attending lectures, watching recordings, reading slides, highlighting notes, asking AI for summaries. These may help. None of them prove that you understand.

Understanding is tested by output:

- solving problems without looking at solutions
- writing code from scratch
- debugging your own mistakes
- writing notes in your own words
- explaining a concept to someone else
- teaching the topic as if you were the tutor
- making a small project
- answering past paper questions under time pressure

If you cannot output, you probably do not own the knowledge yet.

> Familiarity is not understanding.  
> Output is where understanding becomes visible.

## 3. Notes Are Not Decoration

You need a note system, not a note museum.

A note museum looks organised, but it does not help you think. It has polished pages, copied summaries, AI-generated explanations, and little actual understanding. It feels productive because it looks clean. Then exam season arrives, and the museum is closed.

The purpose of notes is simple:

> Help your future self restart thinking quickly.

My preferred tool is **Notion**. Not because it is perfect, but because it is practical.

LaTeX is powerful for formal mathematical writing and reports, but too heavy for daily course notes. iPad handwriting is flexible, but handwritten notes can become hard to search, reorganise, and reuse.

Notion sits in the middle: structured enough for courses, topics, links, code snippets, resources, and reflections; lightweight enough for daily use.

A useful note should include:

- key definitions
- important examples
- your own explanation
- mistakes you made
- questions you still have
- links to slides, assignments, or code
- exam or assignment patterns
- things seniors warned you about

Do not copy everything from the lecture. That is transcription, not understanding.

Do not let AI write all your notes either. AI can polish, reorganise, translate, or explain. But the first layer of understanding should pass through your own brain.

> Notes are not transcripts.  
> Notes are reconstructed understanding.

Minimum standard: after reading your notes two weeks later, you should know what the topic was, why it mattered, and what you were confused about.

## 4. Lectures: Attend the Useful Ones, Skip the Useless Ones

Many lectures are bad.

Not all of them. Some lecturers explain intuition, work through examples, give hints, answer questions, or connect ideas beyond the slides. Attend those.

But many lectures are PowerPoint reading sessions. The slides are online. The lecturer reads them aloud. You sit there for two hours and leave with almost the same understanding, only with less energy.

This is not a moral failure. It is a bad learning input.

The question is not:

> Am I a good student if I attend every lecture?

The better question is:

> Is this lecture the best use of my learning time?

For many major courses, attendance may not be strictly required. If a lecture is useless, skipping it can be reasonable — but only if you replace it with real study:

- read the slides carefully
- watch recordings at your own pace
- find external learning resources
- do tutorial questions
- write code
- review notes
- ask classmates or seniors
- use AI to clarify concepts
- attend a better tutorial

Skipping a bad lecture is strategy.  
Skipping and doing nothing is delayed suffering.

General Education courses are different. Many have attendance, participation marks, in-class quizzes, presentations, or group activities. For GE courses, check grading rules before skipping.

A practical rule:

> For major courses, optimise for learning.  
> For GE courses, check attendance first.

Attend the first two or three weeks before judging. Lecturers change. Course arrangements change. Your own background also matters.

Before skipping regularly, check:

- Is attendance recorded?
- Are there in-class quizzes?
- Does participation count?
- Are recordings available?
- Are tutorials more useful than lectures?
- Can you realistically self-study this course?

Do not worship lectures. Evaluate them.

## 5. Course Intelligence: Study the Course Before You Study for the Course

Every course has two versions.

The official version includes the course outline, learning outcomes, lecture slides, textbook, grading scheme, and assessment dates.

The real version includes what is actually tested, which assignments consume the most time, whether tutorials are useful, whether attendance matters, whether past papers help, whether the lecturer gives hints, and which weeks are historically painful.

You need both versions.

This is course intelligence.

Course intelligence is not cheating. It is understanding the structure of the game before throwing your life into it.

Different courses need different strategies. A course with 10% weekly quizzes is not studied like a course with a 70% final exam. A course with programming assignments is not studied like a course with essays.

Before Week 3, find out:

- What are the grading components?
- Is attendance required?
- How heavy are the assignments?
- Are labs graded strictly?
- Is the final exam the main killer?
- Are recordings available?
- Are tutorials useful?
- Do past papers exist?
- Is the textbook actually needed?
- Do seniors have warnings?

Ask seniors, but do not blindly obey them. Courses change. Lecturers change. Assessment formats change.

Use senior advice as intelligence. Then verify it with the current course arrangement.

> Study the course before you study for the course.

Minimum standard: by the end of the first few weeks, you should know what matters, what can be skipped, what is dangerous, and when the real workload will arrive.

## 6. Schedule Control Is a Survival Skill

University gives you freedom. Freedom without schedule control becomes chaos.

Unlike secondary school, nobody will constantly remind you what is due next. If every deadline only exists in your head, your head will eventually betray you.

You need one trusted system.

If you are in the Apple ecosystem, a simple setup is:

> Apple Calendar + Apple Reminders

You can import your timetable from the Student Class Timetable app. Tasks with dates and times can then be managed through Reminders.

The exact app does not matter. The trusted system does.

The key rules are simple:

- record every deadline
- keep tasks outside your brain
- check your schedule daily

Writing deadlines down but never looking at them again is not schedule control. It is digital decoration.

> Your brain is for thinking, not for remembering every deadline.

Minimum standard: if someone asks what is due this week, you should not need to panic-scroll through five platforms and three group chats.

## 7. Read Emails and Announcements Like an Adult

University administration will not always chase you.

Important information may arrive through email, Blackboard, Moodle, CUSIS, department announcements, course websites, or messages from teaching staff. Some messages are boring. Some are useless. Some contain deadlines that can hurt you if ignored.

Check them regularly.

Many Year One mistakes are not intellectual mistakes. They are administrative mistakes: missing a deadline, ignoring a registration notice, forgetting a form, not reading assessment instructions, or discovering too late that something was announced two weeks ago.

You do not need to love admin work. You only need to respect it enough not to get destroyed by it.

A practical routine:

- check university email daily during teaching weeks
- check course platforms several times a week
- record important dates immediately
- save important links in one place
- read assignment instructions before starting
- do not rely only on group chats for official information

Group chats are useful. They are not official records.

Use them for reminders and discussion. Use official platforms for confirmation.

> Boring information can still be dangerous.

Minimum standard: do not miss an important deadline because you “did not know there was an email”.

## 8. Learn How to Use AI Properly

You are entering university in an age where AI exists.

Ignoring it completely is unrealistic. Depending on it blindly is dangerous.

AI is useful for:

- explaining unfamiliar concepts
- generating examples
- comparing ideas
- understanding error messages
- reorganising rough notes
- polishing English writing
- creating practice questions
- helping you start when stuck

But AI becomes dangerous when you use it to avoid thinking.

If AI solves every programming assignment for you, you are not learning programming. If AI writes every reflection for you, you are not developing your own voice. If AI summarises everything and you never check the original material, you may be building confidence on top of hallucination.

A better relationship is:

> AI should accelerate your learning, not replace your learning.

Bad prompts:

> Write my assignment.  
> Summarise this so I do not need to read it.  
> Give me the answer.

Better prompts:

> Explain this concept with one simple example and one technical example.  
> Ask me five questions to test whether I understand this.  
> Here is my code. Do not rewrite it. Help me locate the bug.  
> Here is my explanation. Tell me what is unclear.  
> Give me practice problems, but hide the answers first.  
> Improve the English, but preserve my tone.

Use AI like a tutor, reviewer, rubber duck, and editor — not as a replacement brain.

Also remember: AI usage is not only a learning issue. It is an academic honesty issue.

Different courses may have different rules. Some allow AI assistance. Some restrict it. Some may require declaration. Read the policy before using AI in assessed work.

CUHK may provide student access to Microsoft Copilot through university accounts. This can be useful, but the tool is not the point. Your method is.

Minimum standard: if AI gives you an answer, you should be able to explain it in your own words before using it.

## 9. GPA Matters, But It Is Not Your Entire Life

People like saying:

> Life is more than just GPA.

This is true. It is also incomplete.

GPA does matter. It can affect exchange, scholarships, internships, postgraduate applications, and sometimes your confidence. Pretending GPA is meaningless is not honesty. It is comfort talk.

At the same time, treating GPA as the only measure of your value is dangerous.

I once heard a professor mention that the average GPA in the Faculty of Engineering was roughly around 3.0. I cannot verify this as an official benchmark, and the situation in IE may differ. Treat it only as a reality check, not as formal data.

Engineering GPA is not always inflated. Not everyone gets perfect grades. You are not automatically a failure because your transcript is not beautiful.

A bad grade hurts. Let it hurt for a while. Then extract information from it.

Ask:

- Did I start too late?
- Did I misunderstand the course focus?
- Did I rely too much on passive reading?
- Did I skip useful practice?
- Did I underestimate the workload?
- Was my study method wrong?

A grade is feedback. It is not a personality test.

The correct attitude is:

> Care about GPA, but do not worship it.

Protect your GPA where it matters. Do not destroy it through avoidable mistakes. But do not let GPA stop you from learning difficult things, trying projects, building skills, making friends, or staying mentally alive.

Life is more than just GPA.  
But GPA is still part of life.

## 10. Mental Health: Do Not Let Admission Results Define You

There is one uncomfortable truth that people do not always say aloud.

Some IE students did not enter IE as their first choice. Some wanted Computer Science. Some wanted another programme. Some arrived here because of admission scores, JUPAS results, internal transfer failure, or simply because the system placed them here.

I am one of them.

I wanted Computer Science. I did not get in. I came to IE instead.

This can hurt more than people expect. It is not only about the programme name. It is about pride, expectation, comparison, family questions, peer judgment, and the quiet sentence that stays in your head:

> I am here because I failed to get into somewhere else.

If this sentence appears in your mind, do not pretend it does not exist. Admit it honestly.

But do not let it become your entire identity.

An admission result is not a personality judgment. It is not a permanent ranking of your worth. It is a snapshot produced by one system, at one moment, under one set of rules.

It may affect your path.  
It does not define your value.

IE is not “failed CS”. IE has its own structure, strengths, weaknesses, pain points, and opportunities. You may still take programming seriously. You may still build software projects. You may still study AI, networks, systems, data, security, signal processing, or research topics. You may still become a strong engineer.

But first, you need to stop treating your major as a wound that never heals.

This does not mean forcing yourself to love IE immediately. You are allowed to feel disappointed. You are allowed to feel frustrated when someone asks, “Why not CS?” You are allowed to compare. You are allowed to think about transfer, double major, minor, postgraduate options, internships, or using projects to build a more CS-like profile.

These are not shameful thoughts.

But comparison becomes toxic when it stops you from moving.

A healthier question is not:

> Would my life be better if I were in CS?

A more useful question is:

> Given that I am here now, what can I build from this position?

Mental health in Year One is not only about deadlines, exams, and workload. It is also about identity pressure, regret, comparison, loneliness, and the feeling that everyone else has a clearer path than you.

They probably do not.

Many students are improvising. Some only look confident because they are quiet about their uncertainty.

If you constantly feel inferior because of your programme, talk to someone reliable: a friend, senior, academic adviser, counsellor, or teacher you trust. Do not wait until the feeling becomes your default personality.

Also be careful with self-deprecating jokes. Saying “IE is just CS reject” once may be funny. Repeating it every week slowly teaches your brain to believe it.

> Your admission result is part of your story.  
> It is not the title of your story.

Minimum standard: do not spend Year One punishing yourself for a result that has already happened. Use the position you have. Build skills. Find your direction. If you still want to move, move strategically — not from shame, but from clarity.

## 11. IE Is Chur. Admit It Early.

IE is not a light major.

The workload can be heavy. Programming assignments, mathematics, labs, projects, reports, quizzes, and exams can arrive close to each other. There will be weeks where everything happens at once.

Sleeping at 3 a.m. is not rare. Being too stressed to sleep is also not rare.

This does not mean it is healthy. It means you should not be surprised when it happens.

Many freshmen suffer more because they expect the workload to be smooth and reasonable. Then, when three deadlines appear in the same week, they think something is wrong with themselves.

Sometimes the problem is not you.

Sometimes IE is just chur.

The useful response is early damage control.

When workload builds up, check:

- Which deadlines are fixed?
- Which tasks are actually urgent?
- Which assignments carry the most marks?
- Which tasks can be reduced to minimum acceptable quality?
- Which groupmates need to be contacted now?
- Which lecture can be skipped and replaced by self-study?
- Which part requires help from TA, classmates, seniors, or AI?

You need to learn triage.

Not every task deserves equal energy. Sometimes survival means deciding what must be done well, what can be done adequately, and what should not consume your remaining life force.

But remember:

> Suffering is not a badge of honour.

Do not romanticise sleeping at 3 a.m. Do not compete over who is more destroyed. If you are constantly unable to sleep, constantly anxious, or unable to function, that is a warning signal. Talk to someone trustworthy.

Minimum standard: when the semester becomes heavy, you should have a damage-control plan, not just emotional collapse.

## 12. Do Not Let OCamp and Societies Define Your University Life

Before university starts, many freshmen are told to join OCamp, meet people, join societies, build connections, and make university life colourful.

Some of this is true. OCamp can help you meet people. Societies can give you friends, experience, responsibilities, and memories.

But they are not compulsory routes.

You do not need to force yourself into OCamp or society life just because everyone seems to be doing it. Some activities are meaningful. Some are simply not for you. Both are normal.

The danger is not joining activities.

The danger is letting other people define what your university life should look like.

If you enjoy OCamp, join it. If a society fits you, stay. If you feel uncomfortable, exhausted, or uninterested, you are allowed to step back.

> Join activities because they add value to your life, not because you are afraid of missing out.

Before committing, ask:

- Do I actually want to do this?
- Am I joining because of interest, friendship, pressure, or fear?
- How much time will this take?
- Will this affect my sleep, study, or mental state?
- Can I leave if it no longer fits me?

This does not mean isolation. You still need people. But a few reliable classmates may matter more than a large social circle.

Find people who make your university life more stable, not more performative.

Minimum standard: by the end of Year One, you should have some people you can talk to, ask questions with, or study beside. You do not need to become a society legend.

## 13. Choose a Laptop You Can Actually Carry

Many freshmen overthink laptop choices.

They imagine heavy AI training, 3D rendering, game development, hardware simulation, and five research projects at once.

Then reality arrives: you carry the laptop around campus every day.

CUHK is not flat. You will walk, climb, and move between classrooms, libraries, canteens, halls, buses, and MTR stations. A heavy gaming laptop may look powerful before semester starts. After semester starts, it becomes a brick with RGB lighting.

For many IE Year One students, a MacBook Air is a strong default choice if it fits your budget and software needs.

It is light, quiet, has good battery life, and is powerful enough for most daily tasks: programming, note-taking, reports, reading, browsing, and general coursework.

You probably do not need a heavy GPU laptop in Year One. If you need GPU power later, use lab machines, remote servers, cloud services, or dedicated workstations.

Mac is not perfect. Some software may work better on Windows, and some courses may assume a Windows-based setup. For example, if a course requires WSL2 on Windows, Mac users may need to set up a Linux virtual machine or find another compatible workflow.

But for daily student life, portability matters more than many freshmen expect.

A laptop is not only a computer.  
It is something you carry.

Choose accordingly.

## 14. What Year One Should Really Give You

By the end of Year One, you may not know much advanced Information Engineering yet.

That is fine.

You are not expected to become a network engineer, AI researcher, software architect, signal processing expert, startup founder, and open-source maintainer within two semesters.

But you should not leave Year One as the same person who entered it.

The real output of Year One is not only grades. It is personal infrastructure.

You should have:

- a coding environment you understand
- a basic version control habit
- a basic debugging habit
- a note-taking workflow
- a schedule control system
- a method to judge lectures
- a habit of producing output
- a basic strategy for using AI
- a way to understand course structure early
- a healthier relationship with GPA
- a realistic sense of IE workload
- at least a few reliable people around you
- at least one pressure release valve
- enough self-awareness to know when you are learning and when you are pretending

This does not sound glamorous. It will not impress people at orientation camp. But it matters.

Most later opportunities depend on infrastructure.

Projects require coding workflow. Research requires self-learning. Internships require tools and communication. Higher-level courses require foundations. Even GPA depends on systems that prevent avoidable disasters.

> Surviving Year One is not the final goal.  
> It is the first technique.

## 15. Practical Checklist

This checklist is not meant to make you perfect. It is meant to make sure you do not miss the basics.

### Programming Infrastructure

- [ ] Set up VS Code properly.
- [ ] Learn basic terminal commands.
- [ ] Compile and run a simple program outside an IDE.
- [ ] Learn basic Git: `init`, `add`, `commit`, `status`, `log`, `branch`.
- [ ] Create a GitHub account and push one small project.
- [ ] Learn how to read compiler errors.
- [ ] Learn basic debugging.
- [ ] Learn basic SSH.

### Lectures and Courses

- [ ] Attend the first few lectures before judging a course.
- [ ] Check attendance policy for every course.
- [ ] Identify which lectures are worth attending.
- [ ] Skip bad lectures only with replacement study.
- [ ] Know the grading scheme before Week 3.
- [ ] Know which assignments, labs, quizzes, or exams are dangerous.

### Learning System

- [ ] Build a note-taking system.
- [ ] Write notes in your own words.
- [ ] Do not let AI write all your notes.
- [ ] Produce output every week.
- [ ] Solve problems without looking at solutions.
- [ ] Explain one concept to someone else.
- [ ] Review mistakes after quizzes and assignments.

### Schedule and Admin

- [ ] Put fixed events into Calendar.
- [ ] Put tasks into Reminders or another trusted system.
- [ ] Record every deadline.
- [ ] Check your schedule daily.
- [ ] Check university email regularly.
- [ ] Check course platforms regularly.
- [ ] Read assignment instructions before starting.
- [ ] Use group chats for discussion, not official confirmation.

### AI Usage

- [ ] Use AI to explain concepts.
- [ ] Use AI to generate examples.
- [ ] Use AI to test your understanding.
- [ ] Use AI to review writing and debug with guidance.
- [ ] Check course policy before using AI in assessed work.
- [ ] Do not use AI as a replacement brain.
- [ ] Make sure you can explain any AI-assisted answer you use.

### GPA, Mental Health, and Workload

- [ ] Know that GPA matters, but it is not your whole life.
- [ ] Review bad grades as feedback, not identity judgment.
- [ ] Do not define yourself by admission results.
- [ ] Stop repeating self-deprecating jokes until you believe them.
- [ ] Use early damage control during heavy weeks.
- [ ] Practise triage when deadlines collide.
- [ ] Do not romanticise burnout.
- [ ] Find one place or routine that helps you breathe.
- [ ] Talk to someone when stress becomes unmanageable.

### Hardware and Student Life

- [ ] Choose a laptop you can actually carry.
- [ ] Prefer portability over unnecessary power.
- [ ] Consider MacBook Air if you have no special requirement.
- [ ] Keep chargers, adapters, and storage organised.
- [ ] Do not join OCamp or societies only because of pressure.
- [ ] Try activities if you are genuinely interested.
- [ ] Check the time cost before committing.
- [ ] Keep a few reliable people you can talk to.
- [ ] Leave activities that consistently damage your study, sleep, or mental state.

## Final Note

Year One is not where everything becomes clear.

It is where you discover that the system is incomplete, lectures vary in quality, programming is more than syntax, deadlines multiply quietly, GPA matters but does not define your life, social pressure is real, admission results can hurt, IE is chur, and freedom is heavier than it looks.

Do not wait for the curriculum to give you everything. It will not.

Build your workflow. Learn the tools nobody formally teaches. Skip bad inputs, not learning. Produce output. Control your schedule. Use AI carefully. Ask seniors, but verify what they say. Join activities only if they fit you. Care about GPA, but do not worship it. Stop punishing yourself for an admission result. Find somewhere to breathe.

Survive first.
