# How to Use This Guide

This guide is not meant to be read like a textbook, from the first page to the last.

It is not a syllabus. It is not a checklist. It is not a shortcut through Information Engineering.

It is a field guide.

Use it when you need orientation: before entering a new subject, while struggling through a difficult course, after finishing a project, or when you are trying to understand what your next step should be.

Different students will need different parts of this guide at different times.

If you are an incoming student, start with the chapters on Year One, programming foundations, and common misconceptions.

If you are already taking core courses, read the sections related to data structures, algorithms, systems, mathematics, and project work.

If you are thinking about research, internships, or career planning, read the later chapters after you have built some technical foundation.

You do not need to understand everything immediately.

In fact, you probably should not.

Some chapters are meant to be read before the experience. Some are meant to be understood only after you have been confused, stuck, or humbled by the work itself.

The goal is not to finish this guide quickly.

The goal is to use it as a map.

A map does not walk the road for you. It does not remove the slope, the fog, or the wrong turns. But it can help you understand where you are, what lies ahead, and why certain difficulties are normal.

Use this guide to locate yourself. Then keep moving.

## Do Not Treat This as Official Advice

This guide is not an official university document.

It does not replace:

- Course outlines
- Academic advising
- Department announcements
- Graduation requirements
- Instructor instructions
- Professional or legal advice

When official information matters, always check official sources.

This guide may help you interpret the path, but it does not define the rules of the path.

## Read Actively

Do not read this guide passively.

Do not treat every sentence as an instruction.

When reading, ask yourself:

- Does this apply to my situation?
- What assumptions does this advice make?
- What stage was the author thinking about?
- What should I try next?
- What do I disagree with?
- What would I tell a future student after my own experience?

A guide is most useful when it helps you think more clearly, not when it tells you what to do blindly.

If a section gives you an answer, test it against your own situation.

If a section gives you a warning, ask whether you are already walking toward that mistake.

If a section feels irrelevant, leave it for now. It may become useful later.

## Revisit Later

Some parts will not make sense immediately.

That is normal.

A chapter about projects may feel abstract before you have written enough code. A chapter about research may feel distant before you have taken enough core courses. A chapter about career planning may feel unnecessary until you start looking for internships.

You do not need to understand everything at once.

Come back later.

The same chapter may become more useful after one semester, one project, one difficult course, one internship application, or one failed attempt.

That is not a problem.

That is how a field guide is supposed to work.

You do not read a map once and throw it away. You return to it whenever the terrain changes, whenever you lose direction, and whenever the road becomes harder than expected.
