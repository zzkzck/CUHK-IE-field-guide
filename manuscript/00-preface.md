# Preface

Information Engineering has official study plans, course descriptions, and degree requirements. What it often lacks is something more informal, more honest, and sometimes more useful: a map written by students who have already walked part of the road.

That absence is where this guide began.

For many IE students, the official path is visible, but the actual terrain is not. A study plan can tell you which courses to take. It cannot always tell you what those courses feel like, why they matter, how they connect, or where students usually get lost.

Students need more than a list of requirements. They need warnings before the difficult parts, context before the abstract parts, and a way to understand confusion before they mistake it for failure.

This guide exists because that kind of knowledge should not remain scattered in private conversations, forgotten chat histories, or advice passed down only by chance.

**The IE Field Guide** is an attempt to collect, organize, and preserve that knowledge for students studying Information Engineering.

It is not a textbook. It is not an official curriculum. It is not a universal formula for success.

It is a field guide.

It is written for students who are still finding their bearings: students learning to program, struggling with mathematics, meeting computer science for the first time, trying to understand signals and systems, building projects, looking for internships, considering research, and slowly discovering what kind of engineer they want to become.

This project was originally nicknamed **IE 天書**. The name carries a sense of student tradition: something passed around, updated, annotated, argued with, and rediscovered by future students.

The official name is now **The IE Field Guide**.

A field guide is not written for people sitting comfortably in a classroom. It is written for people moving through uncertain ground.

That is the spirit of this project.

This guide is written with AI assistance and human editorial control. AI may help with brainstorming, rewriting, proofreading, and improving clarity, but the direction, judgment, and responsibility remain with the human author.

If this guide helps even one student feel less lost, ask sharper questions, avoid avoidable mistakes, or build stronger foundations, then it has already served its purpose.

If no map exists, we start drawing one.
