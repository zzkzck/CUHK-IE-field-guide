# What Is Information Engineering?

Information Engineering is difficult to define in one sentence.

That difficulty is not a weakness of the field. It is part of the field.

IE sits between several traditions: computer science, electrical engineering, communications, signal processing, data, systems, networks, security, and increasingly artificial intelligence.

A simple way to think about it is:

> Information Engineering studies how information is represented, processed, transmitted, analyzed, protected, and used.

This includes questions such as:

- How do computers store and process information?
- How do networks transmit data?
- How do signals carry meaning?
- How do algorithms solve problems?
- How do systems scale?
- How do we build reliable and useful software?
- How do mathematical tools support engineering decisions?
- How do we protect information and systems from failure or attack?

For a student, IE may feel confusing at first because it does not belong to only one category.

It is not purely programming.  
It is not purely hardware.  
It is not purely mathematics.  
It is not purely communication theory.

Instead, IE is built from the intersections between these areas.

That can be frustrating in the beginning. One course asks you to write code. Another asks you to prove things. Another asks you to understand signals, circuits, systems, or networks. At first, the pieces may not seem to fit.

But that mixture is also what makes IE powerful.

IE trains you to think about information not only as code, data, signals, or theory, but as something that moves through real systems under real constraints.

That is the first thing to understand.

IE is not a single narrow skill.

It is a way of thinking across layers.

## IE Is Not Just Programming

Many students first encounter engineering through programming.

This is natural. Programming gives fast feedback: you write code, run it, and see whether it works. For many students, it is the first place where abstract ideas become visible.

But Information Engineering is broader than programming.

Programming is a tool. It is an important tool, but it is not the whole field.

IE also asks questions about information, systems, signals, networks, mathematics, security, and design. A good IE student gradually learns how these pieces connect.

This does not mean every IE student must become the same kind of programmer.

Some students may later focus on systems, data, networks, security, theory, hardware-related areas, applications, or research.

But programming is hard to avoid completely.

Many IE ideas eventually need to be tested, implemented, simulated, measured, or communicated through code.

If you are weak at programming, do not panic. It can be trained.

But do not pretend it does not matter.

## IE Is Not Just Theory

At the same time, IE is not only theory.

Mathematics and theoretical concepts matter because they help us reason clearly. They give us language for uncertainty, structure, optimization, signals, information, algorithms, and systems.

But engineering also requires implementation, testing, debugging, communication, and judgment under constraints.

A useful way to think about IE is:

> Theory explains what is possible. Engineering asks what can be built reliably.

Both sides matter.

If you only know theory, you may struggle to build things that work in practice.

If you only know how to code, you may struggle to understand why a method works, when it fails, or how to improve it.

IE often asks students to move between these two modes of thinking.

That movement is uncomfortable at first.

But it is also where real engineering judgment begins.

## Why IE Can Feel Confusing

IE can feel confusing because students are asked to learn many different things at once:

- Programming
- Calculus
- Linear algebra
- Probability
- Circuits or signals
- Data structures
- Systems
- Communication concepts
- Security concepts
- Project work

At first, these topics may seem disconnected.

A programming assignment may feel unrelated to a signals course. A mathematics course may feel far away from software engineering. A networking course may seem abstract until you see how real systems depend on it.

This confusion is normal.

It does not always mean you are bad at IE.

Often, it means you have not yet seen enough connections.

The danger is not confusion itself.

The danger is staying confused without building structure.

IE will keep giving you new topics. If you treat every course as a separate island, the major will feel fragmented. If you start asking how the pieces connect, the field slowly becomes readable.

Over time, the pieces start forming a larger picture.

You begin to see that data needs representation, algorithms need mathematics, systems need architecture, networks need protocols, signals need models, and engineering decisions usually involve trade-offs.

You are not only learning topics.

You are learning how the topics speak to each other.

This guide exists partly to make that picture easier to see.

## IE in CUHK

At CUHK, Information Engineering is not simply a renamed computer science programme, nor is it a traditional electrical engineering programme with some programming added.

The official description presents IE as a multidisciplinary programme integrating computer science, electronic engineering, data science, information security, information theory, telecommunications networking, and artificial intelligence.

For students, this means CUHK IE can feel broad from the beginning.

You may study programming and data structures on one side, and signals, systems, circuits, or communications on another. You may also meet mathematics, networks, security, software engineering, and AI-related topics along the way.

This breadth is not an accident.

CUHK IE sits near the intersection of CS and EE, but it should not be reduced to either one. Its identity comes from how these areas connect through information: how information is represented, transmitted, processed, protected, and used in real systems.

CUHK IE offers five streams of specialization:

- Big Data: Systems and Applications
- Telecommunications
- Cyber Security
- Networked Systems and Applications
- Information Science

According to the official IE stream information, students may choose not to specialize in any stream, or specialize in no more than two streams, subject to the official programme requirements.

Based on the guidance given during Consulting Day, Big Data and Cyber Security are commonly chosen streams among IE students. Information Science was introduced as a possible path for students who prefer a more theoretical direction, want something less software-heavy, or are considering further study.

However, these descriptions should not be treated as fixed labels.

Choosing Big Data does not automatically make someone a data scientist.

Choosing Cyber Security does not automatically make someone a security engineer.

Choosing Information Science does not mean someone is avoiding practical work.

A stream is only a structure.

What matters more is what you actually learn, build, and understand.

For this reason, students should always check the official study plan and stream requirements before making academic decisions.

This chapter only gives a first map of what IE is. Choosing streams, planning courses, and thinking about career paths deserve more detailed discussion later.

## For DSE Students: What Subjects Should I Choose?

If you are still in secondary school and are considering Information Engineering, your DSE subject choices can affect both admission and preparation.

First, always check the official admission requirements yourself. Admission rules, subject weightings, and preferred subjects may change from year to year, and this guide should not be treated as an official admissions document.

From a preparation point of view, my recommended combination is:

- Physics
- Mathematics Extended Module 2
- Information and Communication Technology

This is not the only possible combination.

It is not an official requirement.

It is simply the combination I would recommend to a secondary school student who is seriously considering IE and wants to build useful foundations early.

Physics is useful because IE is not only about software.

Signals, circuits, communication systems, hardware concepts, and physical intuition appear in different parts of the curriculum. Even if you later focus more on software, data, or security, having some physics background can make the engineering side of IE less unfamiliar.

M2 is useful because IE relies heavily on mathematical thinking.

Calculus, linear algebra, probability, signals, optimization, algorithms, and machine learning all become easier to approach if you are not afraid of mathematics. M2 does not make university mathematics easy, but it gives you a stronger starting point.

ICT is useful because it gives early exposure to computing concepts.

It may help you become more comfortable with programming, databases, systems, and the basic language of technology. However, ICT should not be mistaken for the whole of IE. University-level programming and computer science will still require much deeper work.

If you can handle this combination well, Physics + M2 + ICT gives a balanced foundation:

- Physics prepares you for the engineering and signal side.
- M2 prepares you for the mathematical side.
- ICT prepares you for the computing and application side.

That said, subject choice should also be realistic.

Do not choose a subject only because it looks useful on paper. If a subject seriously weakens your overall DSE performance, the cost may be too high. A strong grade in a slightly less ideal subject may sometimes be better than a weak grade in a “recommended” subject.

The minimum standard is this:

Choose subjects that keep the door to IE open, build useful foundations, and give you a realistic chance to perform well.

If you are strong enough, I recommend Physics, M2, and ICT.
